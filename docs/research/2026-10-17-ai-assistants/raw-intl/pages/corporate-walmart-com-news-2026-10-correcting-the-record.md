# Correcting the Record: We Price Products, Not People

Dear Ms. Owens:

Your recent public statements repeatedly mischaracterize Walmart’s pricing practices and use of technology. They leave audiences believing Walmart uses or is preparing to use customers’ personal information or changes in the weather to charge them more. Your account does not accurately describe our practices or our plans. So, we are asking you to correct the record.

Our business is built on trust, which is why accuracy matters. We welcome scrutiny, but criticism must be grounded in fact, not conjecture. Work presented as research carries a basic responsibility to distinguish evidence from speculation and potential technological capabilities from actual business practices. Your descriptions of Walmart repeatedly blur those distinctions, giving people a misleading picture of what they pay and why.

The facts are straightforward. We price products, not people. Walmart does not and will not use an individual customer’s personal information, income, shopping history, urgency or willingness to pay to set an individualized price. We do not engage in dynamic pricing, raising prices in response to a hot afternoon or an approaching snowstorm, for example.

Your public comments suggest otherwise.

You have inaccurately described Walmart’s rollout of digital shelf labels as part of an infrastructure for “real-time dynamic pricing” and connected this rollout to the possibility of higher prices for ice cream on hot days and soup during snowstorms. You framed these examples as what the technology could enable, but repeatedly using Walmart to illustrate them implies this is where our business is headed. It is not. Digital shelf labels replace paper tags and allow approved price changes to be displayed more efficiently and consistently. They do not identify customers, nor do they independently set prices. The ability to update a display more quickly is not evidence that Walmart uses it, nor intends to use it, for dynamic pricing. Walmart does not and will not use digital shelf labels for individual or dynamic pricing.

Another example is your reliance on Walmart patents as evidence that we will pursue individualized or dynamic pricing. Again, we do not and have committed not to. A patent describes an invention and potential capabilities. It does not mean that Walmart has deployed a capability, used it with customers or decided to do so in the future. Those conclusions require evidence beyond a patent. The tuna-and-mayonnaise example illustrates the problem. Putting tuna in a customer’s cart does not cause Walmart to charge that customer more for mayonnaise. We do not do that, and we will not.

Your comments about Sparky also draw unfounded conclusions. We do not use AI to upcharge our customers. Still, you have said customers who use Sparky spend 35% more than those who do not, in part because Sparky is “bidding up their cart total.” In an interview, you acknowledged that differences between customers could help explain the higher basket totals, but nonetheless you connected Sparky to price gouging and overcharging.

Higher average order values do not, on their own, establish that Sparky caused customers to spend more on individual items. They certainly do not establish that customers were charged higher prices for the same items. Sparky helps customers find, compare and discover products. Information customers share with us is not used to raise their individual prices. Telling Sparky that you need something urgently does not cause Walmart to charge you more for it.

Helping people should be the point of this discussion. Unfounded accusations do not help families make their money go further. They give people already worried about affording groceries another reason to worry, without the facts to back it up. We are working every day to help those families save money and live better. Hold us accountable to that promise, but do so based on what we actually do and not what you’ve decided we could possibly do.

If you are seriously interested in learning more about what we do, we would be open to a conversation. Regardless of whether or not you accept that offer, we ask you to publicly correct these mischaracterizations and clearly share our practices and commitments with the audiences who heard them.

Customers deserve an accurate account of how Walmart serves them, not speculation. We expect your future statements to reflect these facts.

Dan Bartlett

Executive Vice President, Corporate Affairs, Walmart Inc.
